Best Practices for
ACH Electronic
Payments

Governments may pay vendors and
employees electronically in one to three
days using the Automated Clearing House
(ACH) payment network. If you use this
payment method, you need strong internal
controls to ensure your money goes to the
right place, because otherwise you may
lose it. Bad actors have impersonated
vendors and employees to trick accounting
staff into changing banking information.
Employees have also stolen ACH payments
by altering banking information.
We recommend that you implement
strong internal controls to prevent
ACH fraud; use this guide to help you
understand and apply best practices.

Take these important first steps
  •   Develop a detailed ACH payment policy. Your policy should
      describe the procedures to initiate, approve, execute and reconcile
      ACH payments. It should include your key controls to prevent and
      detect ACH fraud and how employees should respond to known
      or suspected fraud. Limit sharing this policy to those who need to
      know it.

      Municipal Research Services Center (MRSC) is currently developing a
      Financial Policies Tool Kit for ACH transactions. Check the MRSC website
      for this guidance, expected sometime in 2026.

  •   Require training after hiring and annually, thereafter. Employees
      involved with the ACH payment process will need training in social
      engineering and ACH fraud schemes. You should:

         ◦ Teach employees how to practice professional skepticism
           and spot red flags

         ◦ Educate staff on your policies and procedures, how your
           government is set up to prevent and detect ACH fraud

         ◦ Train staff on what information they can share over the
           phone or email

         ◦ Share the consequences for committing fraud


  •   Encourage employees to be on their guard. Employees should
      know that bad actors may impersonate vendors, fellow employees

Prepare your bank accounts for
ACH payments
  •   Set limits. You may set daily, monthly or transactional dollar limits by
      working with your bank. To set the limit amount, consider the amount
      of insurance you have to cover a loss.

  •   Use a dedicated bank account for ACH transactions. Maintain a zero
      balance and restrict this account from processing any other payment
      types. Work with your bank to block any wire transfers or check
      payment processing.

  •   Use ACH blocks. Work with your bank to place blocks on any account
      not authorized for ACH transactions.

  •   Use an ACH filter (also known as ACH positive pay). Use this tool
      to screen ACH activity and block incoming charges before the bank
      deducts them from your account. You can:

         ◦ Set up a list of authorized vendors, dollar limits, start and end
           dates, and payment frequency. Based on these preauthorization
           rules, the bank will allow some transactions to clear, but will flag
           others for your review.

         ◦ Permanently block certain vendors (fraudsters tend to keep
           trying over time).


Segregate duties to reduce your risk
  •   Require two people to send ACH payments. Work with your bank
      to set this up. One employee should submit the ACH payment file to
      your bank. Then, a second person, preferably a supervisor or manager,
      authorizes the bank to release the funds (after verifying the accuracy
      of the batched ACH transactions).

  •   Separate accounts payable/payroll and ACH payment duties.
      Employees who process accounts payable or payroll should not have
      the ability to process ACH payments or alter banking information.

      Some governments hire an outside company to store
      vendor banking information and send ACH payments. In these
      arrangements, vendors sign up directly with the company to
      receive electronic payment. In one instance, the company even
      included insurance coverage for losses.

  •   Separate banking responsibilities and ACH payment duties.
      Employees who may add or edit ACH blocks or filters to bank
      accounts—or are responsible for reconciling bank account
      activity—should not play any role in the ACH payment process.



Protect the banking information
you maintain
  •   Restrict access to forms. Do not place ACH-related forms on
      your public-facing website (such as sign-up forms, or to update
      payment information). Otherwise, bad actors may use them to
      make their requests appear legitimate.

  •   Ask payees to securely share banking details. If emailing,
      ask them to use encryption to reduce the risk of alteration during
      transit. To avoid email scams, even with
      encryption, you should still verify the
      new information directly with the payee
      (see pages 6-8 for more information on
      verification procedures).

  •   Limit access to sensitive information.
      Securely store and protect banking
      information. Limit permissions to view
      or edit this sensitive information to
      those who need it to do their job.

  •   Consider a self-service payee portal.
      You may find a payee portal a good option for vendors or
      employees, if it is secure and you actively manage user
      access. If you use a payee portal, you should:

         ◦ Require approvals or notifications of changes

         ◦ Maintain an audit trail

         ◦ Adopt strong security measures, such as automatic
           log-off after inactivity, strong password requirements
           and multifactor authentication (to verify identity)




                                                               Best Practices for ACH Electronic Payments | 5
         ◦ Install all software updates and patches promptly

         ◦ Perform verification procedures for any changes made
           within the portal (see below for more information on
           verification procedures)

  •   Use secure, read-only ACH payment files. Securely create,
      store and transmit ACH payment files to protect the sensitive
      data. If your ACH payment file is not read-only, you should
      implement a process to detect and review any changes
      made between creation and submission.



Set up a verification process
  •   Assign a passcode or passphrase to payees. Payees can share this
      information as proof of their identity when speaking with your staff.
      However, they must protect it. For example, they should not store it
      online, share it with other employees or email it (unless encrypted).

  •   Require new vendor application forms. In addition to other
      information you might need, ask them to provide contact
      information so you can later verify any subsequent changes
      to their contact, address or banking information. Designate
      who should review this information.

  •   Verify new or changing payee information. Require employees
      to make changes in person, if possible. Contact vendors to confirm
      changes. You should:

•   Contact two people to verify changes to banking
    information. If resources allow, consider contacting two
    different people who work for the vendor to verify changes
    to banking information. This prevents any one person from
    redirecting payment to a fraudulent account. If you do this,
    require the vendor to provide two contacts when they
    submit a vendor application to you.

•   Take additional precautions for high-dollar payments.
    Consider verifying payee information shortly before issuing
    any high-dollar payment, using reliable contact information.
    Then, promptly contact the vendor and confirm they received
    the payment. Lastly, review the ACH remittance receipt from
    your bank.

•   Periodically review vendor/employee master files. Periodically
    review your employee and vendor master files for red flags, such as
    duplicate records. You should also look for any “matching” banking
    information for a vendor and an employee. Some use a background
    computer application to automatically detect matches, even if it
    occurs for only a few days.

•   Follow up when a vendor reports they did not receive payment.
    If your records reflect you paid a vendor, but they report not receiving
    it, follow up immediately. Bad actors may have intercepted the
    payment. Acting promptly can aid in recovery efforts.

•   Analyze ACH transactional data. Use historical and statistical data
    to identify trends or patterns that may warrant further investigation.
    Some governments might have the ability and resources to use
    advanced monitoring tools to analyze activity.

•   Conduct an annual review. Review your internal controls, their
    design, operation and effectiveness. Review your policy. Check with
    your bank, insurer, auditor and accountant for any new ways to protect
    yourself. Review the people authorized to initiate and approve ACH
    payments and decide if you need to make changes. Also, consider any
    new technology or third-party service that might add protection.

•   Investigate failed fraudulent attempts. This is a good time to
    review your internal controls and share with others in your government,
    so that no one falls for a similar scam. (You may have lucked into
    detecting the scammer’s first attempt and want to make sure you
    are fully prepared to detect all future attempts.)

•   Use fraudulent incidents to inform your risk assessment process.
    Analyze and quantify the extent to which your government has already
    suffered financial losses from fraudulent ACH payments to inform fraud
    risk assessments and prioritize and tailor countermeasures.
